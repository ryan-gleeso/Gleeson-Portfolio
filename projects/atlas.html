Project ATLAS

A modular engineering platform bringing a rising display, embedded controls and future robotic tools together within my desk.

ESP32 Raspberry Pi 5 NEMA17 3D Printing Power Electronics Ongoing Project

The ATLAS vision

ATLAS is my ongoing personal project to build an integrated workspace for engineering, electronics and automation. I am developing the platform in phases, building and testing each subsystem before expanding the overall system.

The wider concept uses a Raspberry Pi as the interface and computing platform, with microcontrollers handling individual hardware functions. Planned additions include a robotic arm and electronics troubleshooting tools.

Phase 1 establishes a working physical subsystem: a screen that rises from the desk and retracts when it is no longer needed.

Phase 1 / Working prototype

Motorised rising screen

I built a motorised screen lift using a NEMA17 stepper motor, a lead-screw mechanism and a guided carriage. A XIAO ESP32 controls the movement, while a dedicated 12 V power supply powers the lift subsystem.

This phase combines mechanical construction, voltage regulation, power distribution and embedded programming into a functioning pop-up display mechanism.

XIAO ESP32 12 V Supply 5 V Buck Converter Lead Screw Stepper Driver C++ / Arduino

Mechanical construction

The NEMA17 motor rotates a lead screw, converting rotation into vertical movement of the screen carriage. Guide hardware supports the carriage and constrains its movement along the lift.

I used 3D-printed carriage and mounting components to integrate the screen with the mechanism. Development involved checking component fit, alignment and movement with the screen attached.

The lead screw has an 8 mm lead. This dimension is used in the firmware to convert the requested travel distance into the required number of motor steps.

Dedicated power and voltage regulation

The rising-screen subsystem uses its own dedicated 12 V power supply. Power is distributed into two branches: a 12 V branch for the stepper motor driver and a regulated 5 V branch for the controller and screen.

An adjustable buck converter steps the 12 V input down to 5 V. This allows the motor and low-voltage electronics to operate from the same supply.

  • 12 V branch: Supplies the stepper driver, which controls the NEMA17 motor.
  • 5 V branch: Supplies the XIAO ESP32 through its VBUS connection and powers the HDMI screen through a USB-A breakout.
  • Common ground: Connects the supply, buck converter, controller and driver to a shared electrical reference.
  • Power switching: A latching switch controls the subsystem's 12 V input.
  • Power distribution: Wago connectors distribute power between the components.

I verified the rails with a multimeter before connecting the low-voltage electronics. The input measured approximately 12.03 V, and I adjusted the buck converter output from approximately 5.08 V to 5.00 V.

Embedded movement control

I developed the movement firmware using a XIAO ESP32. The controller sends step and direction signals to the motor driver, with a toggle switch used to command upward and downward travel.

  • Converted the requested travel distance into step pulses using the lead-screw lead and configured steps per revolution.
  • Implemented switch debouncing to prevent contact changes from generating unintended commands.
  • Corrected the direction logic so the switch positions matched the intended lift movement.
  • Adjusted pulse timing while testing the mechanism with the screen attached.
  • Developed W/S keyboard control as a manual testing option.

The build progressed from short movement tests to switch-controlled travel with the screen attached.

Integration and testing

I tested the mechanism in stages, first checking driver operation and short movements, then verifying direction, switch behaviour and movement under load.

Power testing confirmed the 12 V and regulated 5 V rails, followed by checks of the screen power connection and the subsystem's power switch.

A controller was replaced during development after overheating following a wiring short. This reinforced the importance of checking connections and supply voltages before powering the assembled hardware.

Phase 1 outcome

Phase 1 produced a working rising-screen prototype with its own power supply and local movement control. The screen can be raised and lowered using the lift's toggle switch.

The completed prototype brings the mechanical assembly, power electronics and firmware together, providing a physical foundation for the wider ATLAS workspace.
